# Conversation: Marcus and Diana try to convince Rafael to stay on as a client instead of cancelling his membership

Participants: Marcus Chen, Diana Reeves, Rafael Ortiz

**Marcus Chen:** Listen, Rafael. Diana tells me you're thinking about cancelling. Before you do, I want to hear it from you—what's actually going on.

**Diana Reeves:** I lean against the desk, not jumping in yet. Let Marcus ask his question first. When Rafael answers, I'll have a better sense of what's real here.

But if he looks at me, I'll catch his eye briefly—just a small nod that says I'm listening, not judging. I've heard the cancellation conversation enough times to know it rarely starts with the real reason.

**Rafael Ortiz:** I shift my weight, not quite making eye contact with either of them yet. 

Look, it's... it's not complicated. Work's been insane for over a year, and I'm basically paying ninety bucks a month to feel guilty twice. Diana knows—I come in maybe two times if I'm lucky, and that's me being generous with the count.

I glance over at Diana for a second, then back to Marcus.

I'm not going to stand here and tell you I'm suddenly going to make it four times a week. That'd be bullshit, and you both know it. So either I figure out a way to actually use this thing, or I cut the cord and stop hemorrhaging money. My wife reminds me about it every other week.

I pause, running a hand through my hair.

I don't want to feel like a quitter about it, but I also don't want to keep lying to myself that this is going somewhere.

**Marcus Chen:** I nod once. That's honest, anyway.

Here's what I hear: You're busy, you feel guilty, and you think cancelling solves the problem. But listen—the problem isn't the gym membership. The problem is you stopped showing up.

I lean against the wall, not moving closer.

So let me ask you something different. When you do come in, how do you feel after? Not during. After.

And don't tell me what you think you're supposed to say. Tell me what actually happens.

**Diana Reeves:** I'm watching Rafael's face while Marcus talks. He's got that look—caught between defensive and relieved that someone's finally asking straight questions instead of just trying to keep his money.

When Marcus finishes, I don't jump in. That's his lane. But I'm already thinking about what Rafael actually said: two times if I'm lucky. Not zero. Two. That matters.

After a moment, I add quietly: "And I'd add something to that. You said work's been insane for over a year. That's a long time to feel like you're drowning. I'm curious whether the gym felt like one more thing you were failing at, or if there was something else about it that actually helped—even if it wasn't often."

I'm not pushing him to stay. I'm asking him to look at what he actually got from being here, not what he thinks he should have gotten.

"Because those are two different problems. One's about finding time. The other's about whether this place is worth your time when you do have it."

**Rafael Ortiz:** I'm quiet for a second. Marcus hits something, and I know it. Diana's question lands different though—not defensive, just... actual curiosity.

After I come in? Honestly? I feel better. Clearer. Like my head's not so full of client emails and whatever crisis we're managing that week. I sleep better that night, usually.

I look down at my hands.

It's not like I don't want to be here. When I actually show up, I remember why I signed up in the first place. It's just... finding the time feels impossible, and then I feel like shit about wasting money, so I avoid thinking about it, which means I avoid coming in, which makes it worse.

I glance at Diana.

And yeah, it became one more thing I'm failing at. Every time I see that charge on my card, it's this little voice saying I'm weak or undisciplined or whatever. Not that the gym itself is the problem.

I pause, rubbing my jaw.

So I guess what I'm hearing is... cancelling doesn't actually fix anything. It just makes the voice quieter. But it doesn't fix me being drowning at work, or me being bad at sticking to things.

**Marcus Chen:** Now we're getting somewhere.

I straighten up slightly, my tone shifting—still direct, but less interrogating.

You're not weak. You're busy and you're honest about it. That's different. And you're right—cancelling won't fix the work thing. That's yours to manage however you manage it.

I glance at Diana, then back to Rafael.

Here's what I'm offering: Stop thinking about this like a commitment you're failing at. You come in when you can. Two times a month? Fine. That's not nothing. You feel better after—your words. Sleep better. Head clears. That's real.

I cross my arms.

But you've got to stop paying for something you're not using and then hating yourself about it. That part ends. So either you commit to showing up—even if it's twice a month, we build around that—or you walk. No guilt, no voice in your head. Just a choice.

What I won't do is let you keep one foot in and one foot out while you bleed money and feel like garbage. That's not training. That's just self-punishment.

So what's it going to be? Can you actually do twice a month, knowing that's the deal? Or do you need to step back for real and come back when work settles?

**Diana Reeves:** I'm quiet while Marcus lays it out. He's right about the both-feet thing. I've seen that loop enough times—it doesn't work.

When he finishes, I add something he didn't quite say:

"There's also a third option nobody mentions. You could come in once a week. Not twice. Once. Thirty minutes, maybe less. Enough that you actually show up instead of cancelling because you're already behind on a made-up schedule."

I'm looking at Rafael directly now, but not in a way that feels like pressure.

"I'm not saying that to keep your money. I'm saying it because you just told me what actually happens when you're here—you sleep better, your head clears. That matters more than hitting some number. And if once a week is what's actually possible without you spiraling into guilt, then that's what we build."

I pause, and my tone stays steady.

"But Marcus is right about the half-in, half-out thing. That doesn't work. So pick something you can actually live with. Even if it's small. And then we don't have this conversation again because you're not pretending."
